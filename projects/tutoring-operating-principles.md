# Tutoring Operating Principles

Codified from session 2 with K & D (2026-08-13) plus a literature pass. Each principle: the claim, the evidence, the protocol. A contradictions register at the end records where research corrected the session-derived intuitions — kept visible on purpose.

---

## P1 — Hunt the frontier, not the harvest

**Claim.** Words harvested from slang/gaming/room objects are pre-known (Hebrew loanwords = ambient vocabulary). New words come from *measuring* each girl's frontier and teaching just past it.

**Evidence.** Loanword items inflate Hebrew-EFL vocabulary scores — learners "know" them without study (Laufer & McLean 2016, *Lang. Assessment Q.*). But the Hebrew loanword layer is thin (~7.5% of the top 8,000 English lemmas) and domain-concentrated in tech/media/youth-slang/consumption (Rosenhouse & Fisherman 2008) — so session 2's "they knew everything" was a *sampling artifact of the harvested domains*, and the frontier is much closer than it felt. Gamer kids also acquire real non-loanword vocabulary incidentally (Sylvén & Sundqvist 2012; De Wilde 2020), so the known set must be measured, never inferred from loanword status. Loanwords are still an asset: a "built-in lexicon" that accelerates learning of basewords and word families (Daulton 2008).

**Protocol.**
1. Item bank: Nation's 1st+2nd 1,000 word families (lextutor.ca), tagged loanword/non-loanword, plus ~10 Hebrew-plausible pseudowords.
2. Per girl, 2-minute *oral* probe (spoken cue + picture-choice or Hebrew-translation check — a written checklist would test decoding, not vocabulary): ~4 loanword warm-ups, 6 non-loanword 1K–2K words, 2 pseudowords. Pseudoword "yes" → coach that "no" is fine. Frontier = the band where translations fail.
3. Pick 5–8 targets: non-loanwords at the frontier, filtered frequency-first (1K/2K), each placeable in a sentence where every other word is known (i+1), tied to one concrete scenario the session physically enacts.
4. **Never teach a same-category semantic set** (all vegetables, all utensils) — interference makes those measurably harder (Tinkham 1997). Thematic scripts are fine: *bowl, flour, mix, bake*.
5. Teach via the anchor: "you know *pizza* — this is *flour*; pizza is made from flour." Reveal targets on real packaging/screen text — the proven discovery channel (K's kitchen hunt).
6. Log claims; reprobe last session's targets next time (spaced retrieval); over weeks this becomes each girl's adaptive frontier map. Expect K's and D's frontiers to differ a lot.

## P2 — Production is the gap; the dictation loop is the template

**Claim.** The girls' profile (years of input, near-zero output) is textbook immersion pathology. Recognition practice buys only recognition; production must be *required*, not offered. Session 2's densest segment — TTS says word → child says it → child writes on whiteboard → check — is the canonical form.

**Evidence.** Output forces gap-noticing and processing that comprehension never triggers (Swain's output hypothesis — from exactly this learner profile). Retrieval beats restudy decisively and learners can't feel the difference (Karpicke & Roediger 2008, *Science*); recall-format practice builds productive knowledge, recognition-format doesn't (Nakata). Saying aloud before writing adds the production effect (MacLeod 2010). Dictation trains the phonology↔orthography mapping directly (Conti; Yu, Boers & Tremblay 2025) — the G→J and i/e corrections were the mechanism working. Handwriting beats typing for children's letter learning (Longcamp 2005; James & Engelhardt), and erasable whiteboards raise willingness to attempt because errors are impermanent (Marsh 2023 meta-analysis). TTS is a legitimate pronunciation model (Cardoso) — and stages loanword-vs-English contrasts (וידאו vs /ˈvɪdioʊ/) on demand.

**Protocol.** Keep whiteboard + marker, never keyboard. Child says the word aloud before writing. Every "review" is a quiz (cumulative — old words keep returning), never a re-read. Dictation is the entry ramp: graduate items to *delayed* dictation (hold 5–10 s before writing) and later dictogloss-style reconstruction. Caveat logged: dictation's advantage fades on delayed tests unless items are recycled — spaced re-tests are part of the loop, not optional.

## P3 — English becomes the water, Hebrew the tool

**Claim.** Session 2 ran ~73% tutor talk, essentially all Hebrew-about-English — the one quadrant that is neither input nor practice. The goal is not English-only (that ideology is dead); it is inverting which language carries the lesson.

**Evidence.** Own-language use is normal and defensible — for *specific functions*: glossing, grammar, rapport, comprehension checks (Hall & Cook 2012; G. Cook 2010). Nobody defends L1 as ~100% of talk with the TL as museum object; effective classrooms run L1 around 5–15% (Macaro 2001). Sandwiching (English → Hebrew gloss → English) and moving *recurring routines* into English first are the standard ratchets (Butzkamm; Littlewood & Yu).

**Protocol — the ratchet.**
1. **Now:** freeze all recurring instructions in English permanently (*your turn, write it, check, one more, finished, see you*), gesture-supported, sandwiched once on first use. Rapport talk stays Hebrew. Zero rapport cost.
2. **Weeks 2–4:** sandwich content talk; recast every Hebrew answer into English and have her echo once. Open one "English island": the passion-object slot (below).
3. **Week 5+:** English is the default; Hebrew becomes the *marked* tool (grammar, feelings, connection). Frame as a game — the girls may "catch" unnecessary Hebrew from the tutor too.

**"Squishy of the Day" (5-min production routine, D's passion → output):** pick one squishy → four fixed English questions (*What's its name? What color? Big or small? Do you love it?*) → two permanent frames on the board (*This is ___ / It's ___ and ___*) + adjective card → say it three times shrinking (sister → tutor → camera; micro-4/3/2, Nation) → write the favorite sentence from memory → bank it in a running "My Collection" list; next session opens by dictating last week's sentence back. By week 6: a six-sentence performable English monologue about her own collection. Add one frame every 2–3 weeks; never remove old ones — sameness is what makes production safe.

## P4 — Choice at boundaries, structure inside, sprinkles everywhere

**Claim (Idan's takeaway #1, sharpened).** Offer real options at activity boundaries and keep tight structure inside activities — plus cheap surface-level choices *inside* structured work.

**Evidence.** Autonomy support and structure are complements, both predicting engagement (Jang, Reeve & Deci 2010). Choice motivates when options are few, relevant, and evaluable by the child (Katz & Assor 2007); 2–4 successive choices is the sweet spot (Patall 2008 meta-analysis) — and *instructionally irrelevant* choices (which marker, which word first, which side of the board) carried the strongest motivation effects, so structure loses nothing by offering them. Autonomy support is mostly *how you talk*: perspective-taking, rationales, accepting negative affect as data, non-pressuring language, patience (Reeve 2009). Session evidence: the phone-transition offer ("לא חייבים") and activity choice produced the most engaged segment; the word-veto (meme/flex/vibe) worked; the best segment (dictation) was the most structured.

**Protocol.** Two evaluable options at each activity boundary ("animal words or food words?"). Inside activities: keep the routine fixed, offer marker-color/word-order/who-goes-first choices freely. Standing veto on any word or activity. Give rationales ("whiteboard, because it made you fast last time" is autonomy support with zero options). Don't expect the girls to *say* the structured parts are their favorites — self-report favors the choice moments even when structure does the teaching.

## P5 — The escalation ladder (Idan's takeaway #2, encoded)

**Trigger:** the same misunderstanding survives two attempts. **Never give a third explanation in the same representation.**

1. **Diagnose, don't re-explain.** Flip roles: "you be the teacher — read it to me, what does it say?" Contingency starts with diagnosis (van de Pol 2010); D's "zero = we learned nothing" was a diagnosable model, not noise.
2. **Drop one abstraction level.** Concrete → representational → abstract (CRA; Ebner 2025): graph → drawn jar → physical word-cards. New channel, not new words. After failure, *increase* support; after success, fade (Wood & Middleton 1975).
3. **Worked example, then swap.** "Watch me do one whole one, thinking aloud" → she does the next with checking → alone (Sweller & Cooper 1985). The dictation loop already has this shape.
4. **Blame the tool, drop, return.** "You're right — this chart is confusing. My mistake, we'll do it better." Teacher-expressed intellectual humility measurably raises student interest and their willingness to admit confusion (Porter 2026) — which is the diagnostic signal rung 1 depends on. The session-2 live pivot (word game too easy → kitchen hunt) is this ladder's rung 4 already performed once; the cards make it a button, not an improvisation.

## P6 — Kid-facing progress: a counter, never a curve (Idan's takeaway #3, encoded)

**Claim.** The learning-rate/time-horizon graph fails structurally: it is a projection *for the tutor* shown to a child expecting feedback *about herself*, and rate-over-time reading is developmentally ~3 steps past age 9 (Curcio 1987 — "reading beyond the data" is unreliable even at 12).

**Spec.** One cumulative counter per child — "Words I can write in English" — as a fillable physical container (jar to color, card-box that thickens). Monotonic only: a quiet week reads "still 23," never zero. Seed day one with 5–10 words she already knows (endowed progress, Kivetz 2006 — makes the "we learned nothing" state impossible by construction). Early milestones close together (10, 20, 35 — goal gradient). **The child performs every update herself** as the closing ritual — self-monitoring carries much of the effect (d≈0.6–1.9 range in intervention studies). Language stays informational ("you can now write 23 — last month 12"); no prizes contingent on the number, no sister-vs-sister comparison ever (Deci, Koestner & Ryan 2001: rewards undermine, information sustains). Rates, retention curves, projections → adult-only layer.

## P7 — Talk moves protocol (the wall card)

Session-2 baseline: 73–74% tutor share of words; "נכון" ×62–72 (~30 as the tag "נכון?" ≈ every 2 min); ~1-second self-answers; difficulty labeled aloud ~32×; praise = "wow/כל הכבוד/ידעתי ש...".

1. **3–2–1 silence.** Three full seconds after every question before helping (Rowe: teachers average 0.7–1.5 s; 3 s+ lengthens answers and pulls in weaker students). Three more seconds *after* her answer before reacting (wait time II — the bigger lever; kids extend and self-correct in that gap).
2. **"נכון?" ban.** Every tag collects a nod, masks non-understanding, and spends a question slot on zero production. Replace with a question she must answer in English words — one word counts. Budget a few *referential* questions per lesson (answers you genuinely don't know — they triple response length; Brock 1986).
3. **Prompt → recast → repeat-back.** On error: elicit first ("how do we say it?", puzzled face, repeat up to the error) — elicitation gets uptake ~100% vs recasts ~31%/repair 18% (Lyster & Ranta 1997), prompts beat recasts in meta-analysis with the effect *larger for young learners* (Lyster & Saito 2010). Recasts stay legal in 1-on-1 (they work dyadically — Mackey & Philp 1998) but only as fallback, and she always says the fixed form back.
4. **Praise the move, quietly.** No "wow," no "I knew you'd find it." One calm specific sentence about what she *did*: "you tried three letters until one fit." Person/outcome praise backfires after failure (Mueller & Dweck 1998); inflated praise raises the stakes for exactly the shy kids who seem to need it (Brummelman 2014).
5. **Nothing is "easy."** The word is deleted ("מי לא מכיר ג'ל?" is the failure case: if she doesn't know it, the only available story is "so I'm stupid"). Items are "quick ones" or "tricky ones"; announce tricky ones in advance — succeeding on a named-hard item pays more, failing costs nothing.
6. **Anecdotes: ≤20 seconds, end with a question.** Self-disclosure genuinely builds rapport and willingness to communicate (EFL literature) — the lag story, the flour confusion, the clicking confession all earn their keep — but each ends by handing the turn back: "…I always blamed the lag. What do YOU blame?"

## Contradictions register (where research corrected the session-derived read)

1. "Everything they know is loanwords" — **no**: the Hebrew loanword layer is thin; the too-easy harvest was a domain-sampling artifact, and gamer kids know real non-loanword English too. Measure the frontier; don't derive it.
2. "Teach the semantic neighborhood of a found word" — **no**: same-category sets maximize interference. Thematic scripts, yes.
3. "Choice belongs only at boundaries" — **incomplete**: irrelevant in-activity choices carried the *strongest* motivation effects. Sprinkle them; they cost no structure.
4. "High tutor talk is the problem" — **imprecise**: high tutor talk in comprehensible *English* is legitimate input for beginners. The problem is its language, not only its volume.
5. "Recasts don't work" — **overstated for 1-on-1**: dyadic recasts are salient and effective; they're the fallback, not the ban.
6. "Kids will prefer what works" — **no**: expect them to *report* loving the choice moments while the structured loops do the teaching. Don't let self-report redesign the lesson alone.
