# D1 - The 1940s Soldier in a drawn or printed medium

Verdict: the idea holds. First pick = **Option B, "Four-Colour Misprint"** (a photographed panel from a 1940s newsprint comic book, where print artifacts hide the face). Runners-up: C "Passed by Censor", A "Dogface Ink". Biggest catch: one drawn sibling among ten photographic ones reads as a guest from another show unless the whole cast shares one stated rule.

Method note: every factual row was checked by anonymous web search on 2026-11-12 at search-result level (titles plus result summaries). Pages were not opened in full. Confidence tags reflect that. Lines marked (mine) are design judgment.

Confidence key: high = two or more institutional sources agree. medium = one good source, or several secondary ones. low = single weak source or memory.

What made the mix one world (mine, from the table):
1. A stated in-world reason for the difference.
2. One shared format grammar: same framing, same interview setup, same cut rhythm.
3. Shared light and contact whenever two mediums share a frame.
4. One comic voice.
5. Difference as the rule for all, with zero lone outliers.

Advantage of this project (mine): one shot holds one character, so point 3, the expensive one, almost never applies.

## 4. The catch list

1. **Same species?** Risk: collage. A single drawn decade beside ten photographic decades breaks rule 5 above. Fixes, in order of strength (mine): (a) state the rule "every decade appears in its own decade's reproduction technology" and let at least one or two other decades leave plain photography; (b) photograph the print: frame the comic panel as a physical page under lamp light, so the 1940s is still a photograph, of paper; (c) keep the identical confessional framing, chair height and lower-third caption as the siblings.
2. **Artist names in prompts.** A layperson's flag; a lawyer decides.
   - Andersen v. Stability AI (N.D. Cal.): right-of-publicity claims dismissed with leave in Oct 2023; in Aug 2024 a Lanham Act false-endorsement claim tied to the use of artists' names survived a motion to dismiss. Pleading stage, one district court [S54]. Conf. medium.
   - US Copyright Office reports (Jan and May 2025), per law-firm summaries: style as such sits outside copyright; style-imitating output can still weigh in fair-use analysis [S55]. Conf. medium, secondhand.
   - Mauldin's drawings: copyrighted, permissions via Bill Mauldin Estate LLC (Library of Congress rights page, 2008) [S4]. Conf. medium.
   - Sad Sack: Baker sold the rights; Harvey published the comic book [S5]. Conf. medium.
   - Private Snafu: public domain as US government films [S16]. Fleischer Superman shorts: public domain through non-renewal; ancillary rights with Warner [S18]. The Superman character stays protected (memory, medium).
   - US federal posters: generally public domain; privately made artwork inside a collection can differ [S56]. UK Crown works: 50 years from publication [S57].
   - Practice (mine): zero artist names, zero character names in prompts. Replace with descriptors: "1940s frontline newspaper gag cartoon, heavy brush blacks, grease crayon tone"; "adventure-strip chiaroscuro, shadows massed in brush, pen outlines"; "four-colour newsprint, coarse dot tints, off-register plates"; "1940s Technicolor theatrical cel, one hard shadow tone, painted gouache background"; "wartime security poster, flat lithographic colour, bold sans slogan".
3. **Motion in a 3 to 5 second shot.** Three period-true grammars:
   - Motion comic (a still panel with camera moves, pop-in balloons and sound; term popularised by the 2008 "Watchmen" release) [S59]. Zero generative motion needed; an editor does it.
   - Panel cuts: 2 or 3 poses inside one shot, each held. Matches the show's rhythm (mine).
   - Limited animation (few drawings, held poses, one body part moving) [S58].
4. **Easier or harder for AI video?** Evidence is thin. One vendor guide says flat 2D is harder than 3D or photoreal because clean lines and flat fills expose flicker and "line boil" (outlines wobbling frame to frame) [S61]; vendor claim, low confidence. Research papers report that general video models struggle to keep a cartoon's identity and to produce cartoon-style motion [S61]; medium. Judgment (mine): full cel motion = harder than photoreal. Dot screens and crayon texture = highest shimmer risk. Motion comic and panel cuts = easier than photoreal, because the still does the work. Paper grain, dots and stamps belong in a fixed overlay added after generation.
5. **Performance with zero face.** Carriers: voice; helmet tilt and nod; gloved hands; posture; one prop per shot. Comics add: balloon shape (jagged, dashed, wobbly), lettering size, caption boxes for the interviewer, and emanata (the sweat drops, speed lines and puffs catalogued in Mort Walker's "Lexicon of Comicana", 1980) [S60]. Sad Sack ran wordless for years on body alone [S5].
6. **Lettering.** Image models misspell. Balloons and slogans go in as empty shapes; text is set in the edit (mine).
7. **Tone drift.** Rubber-hose cartoon bodies pull toward slapstick; the show is deadpan (mine).

## 5. Seven options

Prompts use positive terms only. Gloves, scarves and high collars cover skin. Each prompt describes the area under the brim as ink or shadow.

### A. Dogface Ink
- Medium: frontline single-panel gag cartoon, black brush and crayon on newsprint (row 1).
- Device: brim shadow is a solid ink pool. Each confessional line is the caption under the panel.
- Joke: the driest format of the decade. Picture says one thing, caption says the other.
- Motion: 2 or 3 panels per shot, hard cuts; caption types on.
- Risk: closest to one estate's signature look; B&W sits furthest from colour siblings; crayon texture boils in generated video.

> Single-panel 1940s frontline newspaper gag cartoon, black brush ink and grease crayon on rough off-white newsprint. An American infantryman sits on an ammunition crate in a muddy foxhole, squared to the viewer, wool overcoat collar turned up high to the helmet strap, scarf, thick gloves holding a tin cup. His M1 steel helmet is pulled low and everything under the brim is one solid pool of black ink. Heavy slashing brush strokes, deep spotted blacks, scratchy crayon tone on the mud and sky, sagging tired posture. Wide blank strip of paper below the drawing reserved for a typed caption. Slight ink bleed, visible paper fibre.

### B. Four-Colour Misprint (medium as gag)
- Medium: a panel from a ten-cent newsprint comic book, photographed as a physical page (row 6).
- Device: the printing press hides him. Stock of reasons: black plate slipped out of register, ink blot, speech balloon, caption box, panel border crop, staple, price burst, torn corner, coffee ring, censor stamp.
- Joke: the Lawful Neutral soldier is failed by the system that printed him, a new way in every shot.
- Motion: motion comic. Slow push on the page, balloon pops in, cut to next panel.
- Risk: species break (see catch 1); dot shimmer if dots pass through a video model.

> Photograph of a single panel from a 1940s ten-cent war comic book, printed on yellowed newsprint, shot flat under warm lamp light. An American G.I. stands at attention in olive drab field jacket, leather gloves and canvas leggings in front of a canvas tent. Bold black brush outlines, flat four-colour inks, coarse visible halftone dots, olive, khaki, brick red and pale yellow. The black printing plate has slipped out of register, so a thick slab of solid black ink sits across the whole opening beneath his steel helmet brim while colour fringes peek at every outline. Empty white speech balloon above, blank caption box in the corner, paper grain, slight page curl.

### C. Passed by Censor (medium as gag)
- Medium: an official B&W press photograph, handled by a military censor (row 15).
- Device: censor's ink block, rubber stamp, razor-cut window, grease-pencil crop.
- Joke: his identity is classified. Subtitles can lose nouns to the same black ink.
- Motion: the existing photoreal pipeline, with the censor layer as a fixed overlay.
- Risk: a black bar can read as a modern crime documentary; keep brush edges and the purple stamp to hold the period.

> 1940s US Army Signal Corps press photograph, black-and-white silver gelatin print with white border, lying on a wooden desk. A soldier in wool service uniform, gloves and M1 steel helmet sits upright on a folding chair in front of a tent, hands on knees. A military censor has painted one neat rectangle of opaque black ink over the area under the helmet brim, brush edges visible, and a purple rubber stamp reading PASSED BY EXAMINER sits at an angle in the corner. Grease-pencil crop marks in the margin, typed caption slip pasted along the bottom edge, soft window light, shallow depth of field on the print surface.

### D. Front-Page Halftone
- Medium: a newspaper photograph in coarse dots (row 14).
- Device: the shadow under the brim plugs to solid ink; fold creases and headlines add more cover.
- Joke: "UNIDENTIFIED G.I." in every caption, for a man who is in every edition.
- Motion: photoreal generation first, halftone filter and paper overlay after.
- Risk: weakest gag engine of the seven; dots flicker if generated directly.

> Extreme close view of a 1940s newspaper front page photograph, coarse halftone dot screen on grey newsprint, black ink only. An American soldier in field jacket, scarf and gloves leans on a jeep bonnet, M1 steel helmet tilted forward. The shadow under the helmet brim has filled in completely, the dots merging into one solid black patch of ink, while the jacket, jeep and sky stay in open, clearly separated dots. Bold condensed headline type cropped at the top edge, narrow caption line beneath the picture, a soft fold crease crossing the page, slight ink offset from the facing page, warm reading-lamp light on the paper.

### E. Shadow Cel
- Medium: 1940s Technicolor theatrical cel animation with painted backgrounds (row 11).
- Device: one hard cel-shadow shape under the brim, justified by a new light source each shot: overhead lamp, searchlight, match flare, projector beam.
- Joke: epic lighting for mundane complaints.
- Motion: limited animation, one moving part per shot.
- Risk: full cel motion is the hardest case for AI video; the look sits close to a famous superhero series.

> Frame from a 1940s Technicolor theatrical cartoon, hand-inked cel over a painted gouache background. A realistically proportioned American soldier in olive uniform, gloves and steel helmet stands in a night-time guard post under a single hanging lamp. Dramatic low camera angle, art deco composition, long diagonal shadows. Clean dark ink outlines, flat cel colours with one hard-edged shadow tone, and the lamp directly overhead turns everything beneath the helmet brim into a single flat black shadow shape. Painted background in deep teal, rust and amber with a soft airbrushed light cone, subtle cel dust and film grain, 4:3 frame.

### F. Training Film Private
- Medium: B&W army training cartoon (row 10).
- Device: the helmet is three sizes too large and drops over his head; signs and arrows label him.
- Joke: he is the bad example in his own instruction film.
- Motion: bouncy theatrical animation.
- Risk: tone turns broad; cartoon proportions sit furthest from the siblings; hardest to animate cleanly.

> Frame from a 1940s black-and-white army training cartoon, rubbery theatrical cartoon style, grey-wash painted background of a barracks yard. A short, round-shouldered private in baggy fatigues, oversized gloves and enormous boots salutes proudly. His steel helmet is three sizes too large and has dropped down over his head so its rim rests on his collar, with a small gap of solid black beneath the rim. Thick confident ink outlines, simple grey cel tones, squash-and-stretch pose, a big hand-painted arrow sign reading WRONG pointing at him, soft film grain, slight gate weave, 4:3 frame.

### G. Security Poster
- Medium: wartime lithographic poster, flat colour (rows 7 and 8).
- Device: the 1942 poster convention itself: a flat black shape under the helmet.
- Joke: each confessional ends on a poster whose slogan is his punchline. Test slogan "KEEP IT UNDER YOUR HAT" is mine.
- Motion: parallax between figure and background; slogan swaps on the cut.
- Risk: static; menace reading (row 7 catch); slogan text must be set in the edit.

> 1940s wartime security poster, stone lithograph with flat silkscreen-like colour, printed on heavy cream paper with pinholes in the corners. A monumental American soldier seen from the chest up in olive greatcoat with collar raised, one gloved hand lifted with index finger upright in front of the helmet, steel helmet rendered in three flat tones. Under the helmet brim sits one clean, flat, solid black shape, graphic and geometric. Limited palette of olive, signal red, cream and black, soft airbrushed gradient in the background, strong diagonal composition, wide blank cream band along the bottom reserved for a slogan, small government printing line at the lower edge.

## 6. Ranking (mine)

| Rank | Option | Gag engine | Species fit | AI-video cost | Legal distance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | B Four-Colour Misprint | 10+ reasons | medium, with the photographed-page frame | low (motion comic) | high (generic print look) |
| 2 | C Passed by Censor | 4 or 5 reasons | highest (still a photograph) | lowest (current pipeline plus overlay) | high |
| 3 | A Dogface Ink | caption format | low | low to medium | lowest |
| 4 | D Front-Page Halftone | 2 or 3 reasons | high | low with post filter | high |
| 5 | G Security Poster | slogan swap | medium | low | high |
| 6 | E Shadow Cel | light-source swap | medium | high | medium |
| 7 | F Training Film Private | slapstick | lowest | highest | high (public-domain series, generic style) |

First pick: **B**. Reasons:
1. It is the one option where the owner's ruling, "a funny reason for the face shadow in every shot", becomes a renewable stock of print faults.
2. The cover (balloon, blot, stamp) can be placed in the edit, so the hide holds on every frame regardless of the model.
3. Colour is allowed by ruling, and four-colour newsprint is the decade's own colour.
4. Motion comic plus panel cuts fits 3 to 5 second shots and sidesteps line boil.
5. "Spider-Verse" already proved that off-register print reads as a style to a mass audience [S51].

Fallback: if B fails the species test, C keeps the gag (the medium hides him) inside the photographic pipeline.

Test (mine): render B, C and A as stills. Cut each between two existing sibling stills. A viewer who has seen none of the briefs answers one question: "same show?" Pass = yes for the three-cut sequence, and the hidden face reads as a joke within two seconds.
